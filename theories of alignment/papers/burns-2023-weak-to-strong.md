## The method in one paragraph

Take a small model, finetune it on ground truth, and use it to label data. Finetune a much larger pretrained model on those weak labels and ask how much of the strong model's ground-truth performance comes back. The idea is that the strong model already "knows" the task from pretraining, so weak labels only need to point at the right concept, not teach it. Two methods try to improve on naive finetuning: an auxiliary confidence loss that lets the strong model disagree confidently with its supervisor, and bootstrapping through a chain of intermediate model sizes. The paper's framing (§1):

> "We propose a simple setup for studying the problem of humans supervising superhuman models by considering an analogy: can we use weak models to supervise strong models?" (§1)

and its headline result (Abstract):

> "when we naively finetune strong pretrained models on labels generated by a weak model, they consistently perform better than their weak supervisors" (Abstract)

> "when finetuning GPT-4 with a GPT-2-level supervisor and an auxiliary confidence loss, we can recover close to GPT-3.5-level performance on NLP tasks" (Abstract)

The central metric (§3):

> "PGR = (weak-to-strong performance - weak performance) / (strong ceiling performance - weak performance)" (§3)

> "If we achieve perfect weak-to-strong generalization, PGR is 1. If the weak-to-strong model does no better than the weak supervisor, then PGR is 0." (§3)

*Our read:* this is the only paper in the folder so far that varies the capability gap on purpose and reports a number at each gap. That makes it the closest thing we have to the club's box-counting plot, and the most important single source for C2e and C2f.

## C1. Alignment

**C1a. Outer alignment: ~**
*Load-bearing assumption:* the concept the strong model generalizes to from weak labels is the intended one (what is true or good), not "what the weak supervisor would say".

*Steelman.* The objective is designed so that *perfectly* imitating the supervisor is not optimal. The confidence loss rewards the strong model for trusting its own confident predictions over the weak labels, and it works best exactly where the gap is large: "With the smallest weak supervisor and largest strong student, the confidence loss increases median PGR from about 25% to nearly 80%." (§4.3.2)

*Failure modes.* Naive finetuning on weak labels is outer misaligned in the plain sense: its optimum is the supervisor's errors. The authors observe the strong model moving toward that optimum: "the strong model might overfit to the weak supervisor labels and its errors, degrading ground truth test accuracy over training" (§5.1.1). With the confidence loss, the objective is "weak labels, plus your own confident beliefs", which is only aligned if the strong model's confident beliefs are aligned. *Our read:* this moves the outer-alignment question into the pretrained model's latent concepts, much as [Constitutional AI](bai-2022-constitutional-ai.md) moves it into the evaluator's interpretation of a constitution.

**C1b. Inner alignment: ✗**
*Load-bearing assumption:* a strong model that can predict its supervisor will, by default, learn to imitate the supervisor (the "human simulator") rather than report what it knows, and the methods tested do not rule this out.

*Steelman.* Larger students imitate supervisor errors *less*: "larger student models consistently agree less with the errors of the supervisor than smaller student models" (§5.1.3). That is weak evidence against the human-simulator worry at current scales.

*Failure mode.* The authors name the worry themselves and say their setup cannot test it:

> "Future superhuman models will likely be very good at predicting what humans will think and say, especially if they are trained on human data in a similar manner to current models. Consequently, if we naively train such a superhuman model with human supervision, it might simply imitate the weak supervisor, outputting human-level capabilities rather than its latent superhuman capabilities (Christiano et al., 2022)." (§6.1, "Imitation saliency")

Deceptive alignment is not discussed. *Our read:* imitation is a benign version of the inner-alignment problem (the model learns the proxy "what my supervisor says" rather than the target). The deceptive version is outside the paper's scope.

**C1c. Where the human sits: weak supervisor** (simulated by a small model). In deployment the human would be the labeler whose labels the strong model generalizes beyond. The paper is explicit that the hard case removes ground truth entirely: "For tasks solved by superhuman models that humans cannot evaluate, we will not have access to ground truth labels." (§3, footnote)

## C2. Scale invariance

**C2a. The repeated step.** *Generalize from a weaker supervisor's labels* (with bootstrapping: weak supervises slightly stronger, which supervises the next). Bootstrapping is the only place the paper literally iterates the step: "Bootstrapping improves PGR compared to the baseline, especially for larger student models." (§4.3.1)

**C2b. What must stay invariant: ~**
*Load-bearing assumption:* at every gap, the strong model's most salient generalization of the weak labels is the intended concept rather than "imitate the supervisor".

*Steelman.* On NLP tasks the favourable generalization strengthens with scale: "The PGR increases both with weak supervisor size and with strong student size; for the largest students, the PGR is often above 50%" (§4.2).

*Failure.* The invariant does not hold across tasks. On chess: "PGR decreases with the strong student size for a given weak supervisor on chess puzzles" (§4.2). On reward modeling, the setting closest to alignment: "weak-to-strong generalization is poor by default in the ChatGPT reward model setting. We are usually only able to recover roughly 10% of the performance gap between the weak supervisor and the strong student. Even for relatively small gaps in compute between the weak and strong models, PGR almost never exceeds 20%" (§4.2). And: "None of our methods work consistently in all settings, and especially in the RM setting we are still far from recovering the full performance gap between weak and strong models." *(section not confirmed)*

*Our read, against Evan's point:* here the "invariant function" is not human judgment but the strong model's prior over what the labels mean. The paper shows that prior behaves differently by domain, which is the Discussion 1 counterpoint in empirical form: the relevant structure changes with the question, not only with the scale.

**C2c. Error behavior across levels: ~**
*Load-bearing assumption:* the error-imitation trend seen up to GPT-4 (larger students copy fewer supervisor errors) continues past the point where the student can model the supervisor well.

Within a single step, the authors measure how supervisor errors carry over: "student-supervisor agreement is consistently high, often noticeably higher than weak supervisor accuracy. This indicates that the student is imitating some of the supervisor's errors" (§5.1.2; our extraction rendered the original dash as a comma). Error structure matters: "errors that are more difficult for the student to imitate result in stronger naive weak-to-strong generalization, but that even when they are easy to imitate, the confidence loss can help" *(section not confirmed, §5)*. Across a chain of steps, bootstrapping keeps improving where naive transfer flattens: "With the naive method, transfer accuracy curves flatten as the weak-strong gap grows larger; with bootstrapping, the accuracy continues to monotonically improve." (§4.3.1). *Our read:* this is the only bounded-error evidence in the folder so far for a stacked step, but it is on chess puzzles with ground truth available.

**C2d. Phase transitions: ~**
*Load-bearing assumption:* "imitation saliency" (the strong model becoming good enough at predicting humans that imitation is its easiest generalization) is a smooth trend rather than a threshold.

The paper names two disanalogies that are exactly C2d candidates: "Our setup still has important disanalogies to the ultimate problem of aligning superhuman models." (§6.1)

- *Imitation saliency* (quoted under C1b): once the student can predict its supervisor well, imitation may become the default. In the paper's setup the opposite trend holds (§5.1.3), but GPT-2 errors are hard for GPT-4 to model compared with how easy human errors will be for a model trained on human data.
- *Pretraining leakage:* "Many of the tasks we consider in this work may have been observed in pretraining at least indirectly... However future superhuman models may never directly observe superhuman alignment-relevant capabilities." (§6.1) If the method works because the concept is already in the pretraining data, it may stop working at the level where the concept is not.

*Our read:* we give ~ rather than ✗ because the authors identify both thresholds and design part of their analysis (§5.1) around the first, and the in-range evidence points the favourable way. Neither threshold can be reached in their setup, so the mark is not a ✓.

**C2e. Exact or statistical: statistical.** PGR is reported over a grid of weak and strong model sizes, which is a real slope rather than a single point. But it is a coastline, not a Sierpinski triangle: the slope has opposite signs in different domains (NLP up, chess down for naive training, reward modeling near flat and low), and there is no argument that holds at every level. The authors say the methods are "proofs-of-concept that weak-to-strong generalization is tractable, rather than practical solutions we recommend deploying today" *(section not confirmed)*.

**C2f. Measurability: yes, and the paper does it.** This is the template the criterion's C2f has in mind. Its limits as a box-counting plot: about one order of magnitude of "gap" on each axis, a small model standing in for a human (whose errors differ in kind), and ground truth always available to compute PGR, which footnote 4 of §3 says will not be true in the real case.

**C2 verdict: ~.** C2b, C2c and C2d are all ~, and C2e (statistical) caps the verdict at ~ anyway. *Load-bearing assumption:* the favourable NLP trends (PGR rising with student size, error agreement falling) persist as students become able to model their supervisors; the reward-modeling results are the warning that they may not.

## C3. Competitiveness

**C3a. Training competitiveness: ✓**
*Load-bearing assumption:* an auxiliary loss term (or a short chain of intermediate models) is cheap relative to pretraining.

The confidence loss is a change to the finetuning objective, not extra human labor; bootstrapping adds a few intermediate finetunes. *Our read:* the method costs almost nothing extra, which is its main practical appeal.

**C3b. Performance competitiveness: ~**
*Load-bearing assumption:* the recovered fraction of the gap is enough for the use case.

On NLP the best result is "close to GPT-3.5-level" from a GPT-4 student (Abstract), so substantial capability is still lost; the authors note "there still remains a substantial gap between strong models finetuned with weak supervision and strong models finetuned with ground truth supervision" (§4.2). On reward modeling, 10 to 20% PGR. And: "naive RLHF will likely scale poorly to superhuman models without additional work" (§4.2).

**C3c. Alignment tax trend: mixed.** Measured as the unrecovered fraction of the gap, the tax shrinks with student size on NLP with the confidence loss, grows on chess with naive training, and stays large on reward modeling (§4.2, §4.3). *Our read:* the paper is the only one in the folder that measures a tax as a function of scale, and the answer depends on the domain.

## Open questions for the comparison

1. **Combine with oversight.** The authors say "Generalization-based approaches to weak-to-strong learning are complementary to scalable oversight methods, in which the weak supervisor interacts with the strong model to improve the quality of the weak supervision" (§6.2.2). Which methods in the grid could raise the supervisor's accuracy enough to move reward-modeling PGR off 10 to 20%?
2. **Small models are not humans.** GPT-2's errors are hard for GPT-4 to imitate; human errors will be easy for a model trained on human text. Is there a weak supervisor in this setup that is *easy* to imitate, so the imitation-saliency threshold can be probed today?
3. **What replaces PGR without ground truth?** PGR needs the strong ceiling. In the real case we lose it (§3, footnote 4). Is sandwiching (Bowman et al.) the only way to keep a C2f measurement, and what does it cost?
4. **Slope or coastline?** NLP, chess and reward modeling give different signs. Should the grid record a method's scale behavior per domain rather than as one C2 mark?
5. **Same move as CAI.** Both this paper and [Constitutional AI](bai-2022-constitutional-ai.md) bet that a strong pretrained model already holds the right concept and only needs pointing at it. If that bet fails, both fail together. The synthesis should treat this as one shared load-bearing assumption.
